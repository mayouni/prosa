A writing style is the set of choices that makes a page one person's: the words he keeps, the words he refuses, the length of his sentences, the mark he reaches for. In this chapter I show what happens to a style when it is kept by a promise. I read four corrections that I had to make more than once. Then I read the studies that measured why, and I leave you with a count to take on your own work.

## Four corrections, each made again

I write with AI agents: programs that draft with an AI model and act on my files. I tell them my style at the start of every session, in a file they read first. And then the page drifts.

Here are four corrections from the record of my house. Each one is written down with its date, and each one was made before.

**A page lists what the project lacks.** On 8 November 2026, on a screenshot of the Zayn site, I wrote: "Avoid this role of transparency that hurts the project image (negative markeing)! Generalise this issue on all Softanza project sistes because I raised this before but you seem to persist on it". The first time is on record too, on 13 October 2026: "do not let the tendency to be transparent and fair make the statements elusive". Both are kept in `zayn/doc/design/WRITING.md`, section 4, and the rule they became is decision AWF-14 of my writing framework.

**A page tells a story where I asked for a statement.** On 5 November 2026: "avoid the narrative, succinct, non declarative style of writing. Here for example, you describe the grid without citing it, neither in the titles nor in the text." The same file says I had asked for it more than once.

**A dash stands where I write a colon.** I measured my own book of 2020, the one I wrote alone: 15 colons against 0.5 dashes in a thousand words. The Refinement book of 2026 took the em dash as its workhorse. Decision AWF-04, of 11 October 2026, names the cause in six words: "the dash was the Refine agent's habit".

**One thing gets several words.** Fresh readers of my third book's pilot chapter counted them. The book's brief keeps their count: "six words for a finding and three for the drawn interface".

Four subjects. Not one of them is hard to understand.

So why do they come back?

## The pull toward the average

An AI model writes toward the average of what it read. That is a measure, and I give it with its sources.

Reinhart and co-authors compared the prose of people with the prose of instruction-tuned models, the AI models trained to follow instructions. The models use present participial clauses, the clauses that open on a verb in -ing, two to five times more often than people do. They use nominalizations, verbs turned into nouns, one and a half to two times more often ("Do LLMs write like humans? Variation in grammatical and rhetorical styles", PNAS, 2025).

Kobak and co-authors read biomedical abstracts for the vocabulary that AI models use in excess. In 2024 at least 13.5 % of the abstracts carried it ("Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary", Science Advances, 2025).

The pull also flattens voices. Padmakumar and He found that essays written with an instruction-tuned model are closer to each other than essays written alone ("Does Writing with Language Models Reduce Content Diversity?", ICLR 2024). Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha found that AI suggestions move Indian writers toward Western styles ("AI Suggestions Homogenize Writing Toward Western Styles", CHI 2025).

Read that last title again. I am a Tunisian programmer who writes in English, and I teach with the colon, the short verdict and the true particular. The average is somewhere else.

## What a prompt can hold

A prompt is the text of instructions an AI model receives before it writes. My style lived there: in a brief, in a list of rules, in a sentence of the kind "never use the em dash".

The studies say what such a sentence is worth. Rana tested one open model and found that naming a forbidden word in the instructions can raise its chance of appearing ("Semantic Gravity Wells: Why Negative Constraints Backfire", preprint, 2026). Jang and co-authors had shown the weakness on negated prompts in 2022 ("Can Large Language Models Truly Understand Prompts? A Case Study with Negated Prompts").

One instruction, against everything the model read. A weak signal.

I said it myself to an AI model, in the middle of a correction, on 15 August 2026: "This is the power of dogmatic influence you got from your training data." The sentence is printed in the first chapter of *Interfaces Under Law*, the sister book of this one.

And my own record gives the diagnosis in one line, written about the Zayn site on 5 November 2026: "It drifted back each time because it lived only in a promise."

A promise is kept by the one who made it, when he remembers it. A model does not remember: every session starts from the average again. So the correction is mine to repeat, in every session, on every book.

Unless the style leaves the prompt.

## In your hands

Take the last three sessions in which you wrote with an AI model. Read your own remarks, and copy each one that you had made before, with its date.

Then sort them in two piles. In the first, put the remarks a program could decide by reading the page: a word, a mark, a title that is too long. In the second, put the ones that need a reader.

Keep the first pile. In chapter 3 it becomes a file.

But first I owe the field its due. People built instruments for prose long before this book, and chapter 2 reads what each one gives.
